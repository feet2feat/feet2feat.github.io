Overview

Change the foot, keep the controller

Humanoid research usually improves the controller and leaves the feet alone. Feet2Feat asks the opposite question: holding a learned controller fixed, does a human-shaped sole help?

We decode a family of soles from a statistical model of human foot scans, mount each on the same G1 ankle, and compare it with the stock foot under policies trained on other feet, from identical starting states. A flat sole built from the same contact spheres separates the effect of shape from the effect of the contact representation.

Read the abstract

Feet2Feat tests human-shaped soles on the Unitree G1 under shared, frozen controllers in simulation. We decode a family of soles from a model of human foot scans, represent each sole with contact spheres, and compare them from identical initial states under policies trained on other feet. All 23 designs turn in place better than the stock foot on level ground, and 22 outperform a flat sole made from the same spheres. A selected design with a broad forefoot and long hallux reduces yaw-rate error by 0.05 to 0.08 rad/s against stock and about 0.05 rad/s against the flat control on four level-ground cells. It wins all 48 paired episodes per cell on sixteen held-out states under three policies. The gain persists when the box's contact settings and inertials are matched to the sphere feet. Three fresh policies reproduce the level-ground turning gain and the walking-while-turning gains on flat ground and pyramids. Under a third policy family trained to track five human-motion clips, the same sole uses 8% less joint work than stock. On rough ground, a span-matched ring reproduces the wide heel's turning gain, identifying a useful geometric property without requiring an anatomical shape. A separate static press quantifies the contact-area change that rigid soles omit. These results make sole geometry a measurable design variable under fixed controllers.

Six independently trained policies

New policies, same ordering

Three population policies trained from new seeds reproduce the level-ground turning gain against both the stock foot and the flat control. Filled bars average the original policies, open bars the fresh ones; each dot is one policy's mean over sixteen paired states.

Human-motion tracking · 5 clips · 228 paired windows · 3 policies

Less joint work when tracking human motion

A separate policy family tracks five retargeted human clips: walk, run, dance, fight and jumps. On identical motion windows, the selected sole uses 3,081 J of absolute joint work per window against 3,370 J for the stock foot, 8% less, and ends fewer windows early (9 against 15 of 228). It also uses less work than a box matched in contact settings and inertia, and than the flat control.

Takeaway: the saving holds on every clip with all three policies agreeing, and again with policies trained with the sole code zeroed.

Mechanism · rough ground · 48 episodes per arm

On rough ground, heel span does the work

Widening the heel of two decoded soles by 40% cuts rough-ground turning falls from 14 to 3 and from 10 to 4. A perimeter ring with the same heel span and no toes or arch reproduces the benefit, matching the wide heel's speed error within 0.002 m/s. A two-rail comb with the same span tracks speed worse, so layout still matters.

Takeaway: anatomy supplies good candidates, and simple geometric controls reveal which property does the work.

Separate static study · finite-element press

What rigid soles leave out

The locomotion soles are rigid. A separate static press of one decoded sole against a plane shows how much compliance would change: at 172 N, the softest tested sole contacts about six times the area of the stiffest, and the contact patch moves rearward as the arch rises.

Takeaway: sole stiffness is a large, measurable design variable for compliant versions of these feet.

04 · Method

Generate, train on other feet, compare with the controller frozen

Generation, policy training and paired evaluation are kept separate, so changing the foot never also changes its controller.

STEP 1

Generate soles

Decode feet from FIND, a 100-dimensional implicit model of human foot scans, move them along measured anatomical axes, and represent each plantar surface with 23 contact spheres on the G1 ankle.

STEP 2

Train shared policies

Train three PPO policies on the MuJoCo Playground G1 joystick task over seven other decoded soles and the flat control. None of the evaluated designs, and not the stock foot, is in that set.

STEP 3

Compare, then confirm

Freeze the policies, zero the sole code, and evaluate every foot from identical starting states. Select one design by a preregistered rule and test it again on sixteen held-out states.

  • 23 spheres of 2.5 mm radius per sole; shared friction, mass and inertia across decoded feet
  • Stock foot: one 18 × 6 × 1.6 cm box per foot, as distributed with the simulator's G1 model
  • 20 s episodes; turn in place at 0.5 rad/s, walk at 0.5 m/s, or walk while turning at 0.3 rad/s
  • 48 paired episodes per test: 16 starting states under each of 3 frozen policies
  • Minimum effect 0.025 rad/s and three-policy sign agreement; 10,000-draw bootstrap intervals
  • Policies: 512-256-128 MLPs with SiLU, 4×108 steps; no noise, pushes or domain randomization

Population policies · 3 seeds · 4×108 steps

Training curves

Evaluation logged twenty times during training of the three frozen population policies. All three reach a plateau. At the final evaluation every episode lasts the full 1000 steps, with linear-velocity tracking returns of 901 to 918 and angular-velocity tracking returns of 642 to 657.

05 · Scope

What these results cover

  • Everything is simulation on the MuJoCo G1 model; no physical robot was used.
  • Rankings are measured under the stated frozen policy families. Retraining a controller for each foot can change the ordering.
  • The locomotion soles are rigid. Compliance is studied separately in a static press, shown above.
  • Work figures are absolute mechanical joint work, which counts braking as a cost, not battery energy.
